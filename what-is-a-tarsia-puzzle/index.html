What is a Tarsia puzzle?

A set of pieces — usually triangles — that fit together into a larger shape. Every edge carries either a question or an answer, and two pieces may only touch where a question meets the answer that belongs to it. Assembling the shape and answering every question are the same act.

Why it works in a classroom

The puzzle marks itself. A student who has made an error finds that the pieces stop fitting, and goes back to look for it without being told to — which is a different experience from handing in a worksheet and finding out two days later.

It also makes students talk. Put one set between three students and the maths has to be said out loud, because the pieces are shared. Teachers reach for a Tarsia at exactly the moment a silent class needs to be arguing about a method.

And it absorbs practice that would otherwise be dull. Twenty-four quadratics are twenty-four quadratics; as a hexagon they are a puzzle, and students do more of them than they would have agreed to.

The shapes

  • Triangle — small triangular pieces forming one large triangle. The classic, and the easiest to cut.
  • Hexagon — triangles tiled into a hexagon. More pieces, more connections, and harder, because there is no long straight edge to anchor from.
  • Squares — a grid of square pieces. Four edges each, so it constrains more tightly than it looks.
  • Dominoes — a chain where each tile's answer leads to the next tile's question. Good for a sequence of steps, or for a starter.
  • Matching cards — no interlocking at all, just pairs to put together. The gentlest version, and the right one for younger students.

Choosing the size

Enough to be work, few enough to finish. For most classes a 16-piece triangle or a 24-piece hexagon fills a lesson; a small shape makes a starter, and the 54-piece hexagon is a group task rather than an individual one. It is better to run a smaller puzzle to a finish than to abandon a larger one at the bell.

The trick with the outside edges

On a plain puzzle the border edges are blank, which tells a student which pieces face outwards. A class that notices this can assemble a good deal of the shape geometrically, answering far fewer questions than you intended.

Filling those edges with plausible wrong answers removes the shortcut. The original desktop app offered this as one specific template, the "extended" hexagon; here it is a switch you can turn on for any tiled shape.

Printing and running one

  • Print the pieces on card if you can. Paper pieces curl and tear by the third class.
  • Cutting is the slow part. Either make it the first task of the lesson, or cut one class set and laminate it so it survives the year.
  • Bag each set. A single lost piece makes a puzzle unsolvable.
  • Keep the answer sheet. It prints alongside the pieces, and it lets you check a finished shape from across the room.

Making your own

Write your pairs first, in a list, before you think about shapes — the number of pairs decides which shapes are available to you. Then open the puzzle maker, choose a shape, type them in and print it. Changing shape or size afterwards does not discard what you have typed, so you can see how the same content looks as a triangle and as a hexagon before deciding.

Pairs do not have to be maths. Vocabulary and translations, elements and symbols, dates and events, terms and definitions: anything that comes in twos will work.
